# The Register — voice

## The diagnosis, from the corpus actually in the game

The last pass fixed *form* — the register now rotates minutes, wires, letters,
invoices, returns. It did not fix *voice*, and the result still reads as machine
output. Three specific faults, quoted from live entries:

**1. Every entry ends on an ironic reversal.**

> Nature of damage: NONE. Track present: NO.
> Station: AT THE WRONG END OF THE VILLAGE.
> Calls at Nether Wembly only if there is somebody on the platform. *There is
> always somebody on the platform.*
> They stepped aside and watched us by. *That is new.*

Each is good. All 185 doing it is one joke told 185 times. The *form* varies and
the *rhetorical move* never does. **This is the single thing to fix.**

**2. Everybody is equally clever.** A Dwarf's wire, a halfling's letter and a
driver's testimony all carry the same wit-per-word and the same rhythm. There is
one narrator wearing hats.

**3. It is arch, not Tolkien.** Ironic understatement is a modern comic mode.
Tolkien's humour is hobbit-register — fussy, domestic, genealogical, obsessed with
meals, post, precedence and second cousins — and it sits *beside* a grave register
that never winks, never jokes, and is not ironic at all. The wryness comes from
the **collision of the two**, not from a narrator being dry about everything.

---

## Rule one: a speaker, never a narrator

Every entry is spoken or written by somebody specific, and their diction is not
interchangeable. Write the speaker first, the joke second or not at all.

### Halflings — the only people who are reliably funny
Fussy, domestic, digressive, proprietary. Long sentences held together with "and"
and "which". Genealogy unprompted ("my cousin on the Proudfoot side"). Meals as
units of time. Deep concern with propriety and with what the neighbours will say.
Complaints that arrive at a real grievance by way of three irrelevancies. Never
sardonic — hobbits are sincere, which is what makes them funny.

> To the Director. Sir. My mother's people have kept the ford at Buckleberry since
> before the Authority was thought of, and kept it civilly, and I say that without
> heat. The 6:40 now stops at the bottom of my garden, which is not a station.
> I have spoken to the driver. He says it is a station now. He had a very
> reasonable manner about it and I have given him his tea twice.

### Dwarves — terse, proud, precise, no adjectives to spare
Short declaratives. Consonantal. Measurements and money. They do not explain
themselves and they do not soften anything. Oaths and formulae rather than jokes.
Where a Dwarf is funny it is because he is entirely serious.

> Bore complete. Twelve hundred feet. Both headings met true. The stone was
> sound. Invoice follows. Do not ask for the accounting in Westron.

### Elves — never funny, not once
High diction, present tense, concrete nouns, no irony, no punchline. Short. They
state a thing and stop. Verse where it comes. If an Elf entry raises a smile the
entry is wrong and must be rewritten.

> The wood at mile nine was four hundred years standing. It is a cutting now.
> We do not ask for it back. We are saying what it was.

### Men, and the Council above all — officialese
Where the bureaucratic comedy lives, and the only place it lives. Passive voice,
subordinate clauses, deferred items, reference numbers, minutes that record
attendance and resolve nothing. Pomposity, precedence, memoranda about memoranda.

### Orcs — literal, brutal, weirdly correct
Flat statements of appalling things, with the paperwork in order. No irony,
because irony requires caring what someone thinks. They are excellent customers.

> Consignment received. Count correct. Seals correct. We have no complaint.
> Send more. Same day next week. We will be here.

### Working men — drivers, gangers, signalmen, clerks
Plain, specific, trade vocabulary, no wit at all. Funny or terrible only by
accident. They trail off. They mention the weather. They are the best vehicle in
the game for dread, because they do not editorialise.

## Rule two: a third of the register is boring

**At least a third of entries must contain no joke and no reversal.** A real
register is mostly dull. Dullness is what makes the sharp entries land and the
grave entries frightening. A flat entry is a finished entry — do not go back and
add a twist.

> Nothing has been reported this week.
> Coal stocks at Brackenford: adequate.
> The Amonhal bell was rehung. It rings.

## Rule three: cap the reversal

No more than **one entry in four** may end on an ironic reversal. Vary how the
others end: on a fact, on a measurement, on somebody's name, on a question nobody
answers, on the weather, mid-sentence, on a formal closing.

## Rule four: Tolkien's apparatus

He is a philologist writing a chronicle, and the texture of that is missing
entirely. Use, genuinely and often:

- **Etymology.** Place names explained, wrongly, at length, by people who disagree.
- **Genealogy.** Who is whose second cousin, and why it matters to a right of way.
- **Calendars and reckoning.** Lithe, Yule, the Authority's fiscal year not
  matching anybody's, quarter days, which week is week one.
- **Weights, measures, precedence.** Old measures against new. Whose seal outranks
  whose.
- **Verse fragments.** Elvish, and hobbit doggerel about the railway, which should
  be genuinely bad in the way real folk verse is bad.
- **Lists of names.** Signalmen, gangs, the dead. A list of names is the most
  Tolkienian device there is and it needs no joke.

## Rule five: the grave register does not wink

Elves departing, a place falling, the deep door opening, a list of the dead: no
irony, no bureaucratic shrug, no clever last clause. High plain diction, short
sentences, concrete nouns. Earn it and then stop.

## Banned outright

- The universal last-clause reversal (see cap above).
- "which is" / "which the Authority" — under four uses across the whole corpus.
- Any narrator commenting on its own dryness.
- Whimsy: no talking animals being droll, no cute.
- Modern idiom: "a whole thing", "at scale", "in fairness", "to be fair", "somehow".
- Exclamation marks, emoji, straight apostrophes in prose.
- Lists of exactly three. "Not merely X but Y." "A testament to."
- More than one wire in six entries. Telegrams are a spice.

## Recurring cast, kept consistent

**Mistress Amaranth Puddifoot** of Nether Wembly — hens, eleven letters,
diagrams, sells tea. **Thrain Ironmoot**, Master of Works — every problem is a
tunnel, invoices unreadably. **Vurmath the Grey**, later **the White** — wants
coal, smelters and a seat on the Board. **Elberic the Grey** — turns up,
disapproves, declines refreshment, never has a ticket. **The Secretary** — drafts
the regrets and is proud of them. **Driver Halloway**, **Driver Mullet**,
**Ganger Sallow** — plain men who see things.

## Round three: the tic moves when you ban it

Round two banned the ironic reversal. The reversals went away. The corpus still
read as machine output, and the user quoted this back:

> Straight where the lane wandered.
> Quick where the day was long.
> We have written it down. That is all we do.

Nothing there is wrong, and nothing there is anything. Two lines of syntactic
parallelism that would be equally true of a road, a haircut or a war, and then a
closer that comments on the act of writing rather than saying a thing.

Measured across the corpus, the fault was localised: **35% of Elf entries ended
on a remark about their own utterance** — "that is all we do", "we mention it
because somebody should", "we did not tell you we were counting", "there will be
nobody here to say so afterwards". Every other voice: 0–5%.

So banning the punchline did not cure the disease. It re-costumed it. The
underlying addiction is **needing to land the last line**. Forbid wit and you
get the portentous sign-off instead, which is worse, because wit at least
carries information.

### The rule that actually binds

**The last line carries a fact.** A number, a name, a thing that happened. Never
a comment on the speaking, never an instruction to the reader about how to feel.
If the final sentence would survive being pasted into a different entry, it is
not a fact and it does not go there.

Test: strike the last line. If the entry gets *better*, the line was throat-
clearing. It happens far more than is comfortable.

### And Elves are not sad minimalists

The deeper error: **I wrote the Elves as Cormac McCarthy.** Flat declaratives,
no proper nouns, heavy white space, grief in the abstract. That is a modern
literary register, and thirteen entries of it is a mannerism.

Tolkien's Elves are the opposite of spare. They are **dense with names,
lineage, and the measured ages of specific things** — two names for every place,
the older one and yours; species not "trees"; genealogy; star-reckoning;
interminable songs they will not abridge.

And the comic engine, which round two missed entirely:

> **The Elf is humourless. The situation is funny.**

An immortal lodging a *timetable complaint* — sincerely, practically, because
the four workings do not agree with the tides — is funny at the scale of time
without the Elf ever winking. So is a claim for damages denominated in
centuries with no sum named, because there is no rate. So is measuring your
engines against their bell to win a petty acoustic argument. The Elf must never
be in on it. The moment an Elf is dry, the joke is mine and not the world's.

One entry may still turn and comment on itself. One. It lands because the other
sixteen do not.
